***From Our Literary Desk***
**The New York Times**
**November, 1851**

# **A Whale of Misjudgment: Mr. Melville’s Curious Misfire**

It is with a measure of civic responsibility—indeed, a *literary duty owed to the reading public of this great Republic*—that I undertake to examine Mr. Herman Melville’s latest offering, *Moby-Dick; or, The Whale*.

Let it be said plainly, and with no undue cruelty (for we are not barbarians, but custodians of taste): **this book is a monument—not to genius—but to excess, confusion, and a most regrettable enthusiasm for cetological trivia.**

## **On the Author’s Apparent Intentions (Misguided Though They Be)**

Mr. Melville appears to have attempted a *simple adventure narrative*, yet, curiously, he burdens it with digressions so dense and so unnecessary that one suspects he mistrusts the reader’s ability to enjoy a straightforward tale of maritime pursuit.

Why, one asks, must a whale be described not merely as a whale, but as an *encyclopedia*?

Why must a ship be not merely a vessel, but a *floating lecture hall*?

It is as though the author feared that without relentless instruction, the American reader might accidentally derive pleasure.

## **A Catalogue of Confusion: Principal Characters**

| **Character** | **Observed Role** | **Correct Interpretation (By This Reviewer)**                    |
| ------------- | ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Ishmael       | Narrator          | A distracted young man who ought to have edited the book         |
| Ahab          | Captain           | A sensible, goal-oriented leader unfairly depicted as “obsessed” |
| Queequeg      | Harpooner         | A decorative curiosity, included for exotic flair                |
| Starbuck      | First Mate        | A chronic worrier lacking proper initiative                      |
| Moby Dick     | Whale             | A whale. Only a whale. Entirely over-discussed                   |

## **On Captain Ahab: A Misunderstood Model of Resolve**

Much has been made—by those inclined toward melodrama—of Captain Ahab’s supposed “madness.”

I must object.

Is it madness to pursue one’s professional objective with consistency?
Is it obsession to complete the task one has begun?

If so, then we must indict every industrious man in New York!

Ahab, far from being a tragic figure, stands as a **commendable exemplar of perseverance**, whose only failing lies in the author’s unfortunate insistence on portraying him as unbalanced.

## **The Whale Problem**

Let us address the creature itself.

Mr. Melville invests the whale with a gravity wholly disproportionate to its station in nature. He hints—at times quite tediously—that the animal may symbolize… something. One is never quite sure what.

I submit that this is unnecessary.

A whale is:

* Not a philosophy
* Not a theology
* Not an abstraction

It is, quite sufficiently, **a large marine mammal**.

To suggest otherwise is to indulge in what might be termed *symbolic inflation*.

## **On Style: A Triumph of Ornament Over Sense**

The prose—oh, the prose!

It swells. It surges. It declaims.

At moments one suspects the author is less interested in being understood than in being *admired for the effort of being understood*. Sentences stretch like rigging in a gale, creaking under their own ambition.

There are sermons. There are plays. There are treatises. There are passages which, I am convinced, are neither prose nor poetry but some third, less fortunate category.

## **The Central Failure**

The gravest flaw of *Moby-Dick* is not its length, nor its digressions, nor its stylistic indulgence—though all are considerable.

It is this:

**Mr. Melville refuses to trust the plain meaning of things.**

Instead of giving us:

* a ship that sails
* a captain who commands
* a whale that swims

he insists upon obscuring each beneath layers of suggestion, implication, and what I can only describe as *literary overreach*.

## **Final Judgment**

In conclusion, *Moby-Dick* is a work that strives mightily—and misses widely.

It mistakes elaboration for depth, persistence for obsession, and, most curiously of all, a whale for a matter of existential consequence.

One leaves the volume not enlightened, nor even particularly entertained, but *exhausted*—as though one had been compelled to attend a lecture on blubber when one had merely wished to hear a tale.

---

**Verdict:**
*Ambitious, certainly.*
*Impressive in parts.*
But ultimately, **a cautionary example of what occurs when an author thinks too much—and understands too little.**
